Wearable nerve-repair tracking

A compression glove that watches how a damaged nerve recovers, one worn session at a time.

Electrodes built into the glove pick up electrical activity from the hand as a nerve heals, so change over weeks can be tracked instead of guessed at. This page runs the growing set of tests behind that tracking — checking electrode contact, capturing a personal baseline, timing reaction speed, and logging sessions over time.

Five tests, one recovery picture

Contact check and calibration are built and working in the panel below. Reaction-time testing and baseline recording are up next. Together, these are what session logging and long-term recovery tracking get built on top of.

Why every test starts with contact quality

Every reading the glove takes — resistance, reaction time, baseline signal, or anything logged later — rides on top of skin-electrode contact. If that contact is loose or uneven, changes from a shifting glove can look exactly like changes in the underlying signal — which is the one thing you can't afford when the whole point is tracking a slow recovery over weeks. A clean, low, stable resistance reading here means the glove's channel can be trusted; a high or jumpy one means an electrode needs repositioning, a quick skin wipe-down, or a firmer and more even fit before any other test runs.

1

Put the glove on

Once worn, a small known signal passes across the electrode's skin path and the board measures the voltage drop, giving a real-time resistance reading for that channel.

2

Calibrate to your own skin

With the electrodes pressed on in your best, firmest placement, the board records your personal baseline resistance. Skin conductivity varies a lot from person to person, so contact quality is judged against your own baseline — not one fixed number for everyone.

3

Confirm contact before tracking

Each reading is compared against your calibrated baseline and a plain verdict — excellent, good, marginal, poor, or no contact — is sent back over serial before any nerve-signal recording begins.

From a snap electrode to a plotted trend

Three sensors, three electrodes, and one running principle underneath all of it: nothing gets judged against a fixed universal number — every reading is judged against that specific person's own calibrated baseline. Here's how a raw voltage actually turns into something worth tracking over weeks.

Wiring, sensor by sensor

Live

Contact-check circuit

E1→ 5V
E2→ resistor → GND
A0reads E2 / resistor junction

The skin between E1 and E2 behaves like the variable resistor in a standard voltage divider. Better contact means lower, steadier resistance, which shows up as a shift in the voltage A0 reads. REF sits unused here — a two-point resistance check has no use for a third, reference-only contact.

Next

EMG sensor

E1→ module active input 1
E2→ module active input 2
REF→ module reference input
VCC / GND→ 5V / GND
SIG→ A1 (kept separate from A0)

Same three electrodes get reused, not a new set. REF gives the circuit a stable, muscle-free "zero" — ambient electrical noise hits E1 and E2 almost equally, so comparing their difference cancels most of it out, and REF is what makes that comparison meaningful. Running SIG into A1 instead of A0 lets contact quality and real EMG signal be checked side by side.

Electrode placement guide
  • Muscle belly, not tendon — upper-to-middle third of the forearm, closer to the elbow than the wrist.
  • Along the fibers — E1 and E2 sit one above the other along the muscle's length, a couple of centimeters apart, not side by side across the arm.
  • REF on a bony, electrically quiet spot — the wrist bone or elbow, away from active muscle tissue.
  • Same spot, every session — tracing the outline, a reference photo, or a measured distance from the elbow crease keeps week 3 comparable to week 1, instead of mistaking placement drift for real recovery change.
Next

Flex sensor

Leg 1→ 5V
Leg 2→ resistor → GND
A2reads flex / resistor junction

The same voltage-divider pattern as the contact-check circuit, just with the flex sensor itself as the variable element instead of skin. It mounts across the index finger's DIP joint — the joint nearest the fingernail — matching the joint the reaction-time test is built to measure.

All three share one common ground rail on the breadboard. Connections are made with alligator clips onto each electrode's snap button — fine for bench testing, though worth taping down or eventually soldering once real finger movement starts tugging at the wiring.

From a raw number to a recovery trend

1

A raw number, per sensor

Every sensor hands the board the same basic thing: a number from 0 to 1023, since that's simply how the board's analog-to-digital conversion works. What that number represents depends on which sensor produced it — and none of it means anything universal on its own. A raw EMG reading of 600 isn't inherently "strong" or "weak."

2

Calibrate to the wearer

Skin conductivity, muscle output, and finger range of motion all vary from person to person, so every sensor is judged against its own wearer's baseline rather than a fixed number: contact against a personal best-contact reading, EMG between "relaxed" and "flexed hard," the flex sensor between "straight" and "fully bent."

3

Resistance becomes a verdict

The contact-check circuit turns a calibrated resistance into one of five plain verdicts — None, Poor, Marginal, Good, Excellent — averaging 10 samples first so one noisy reading can't flip the result.

4

Two sensors, one timed event

The reaction-time test times the relationship between two sensors: a countdown, a randomly-timed go cue (T-cue), the EMG signal crossing above resting baseline (T-emg), and the flex sensor detecting the finger actually move (T-flex). Premotor time is T-emg minus T-cue; electromechanical delay is T-flex minus T-emg.

5

One run becomes one session record

A finished test's numbers are bundled with the contact quality they were measured under, rather than stored loose. A session measured through bad contact can produce numbers that look like a real physiological change when they're really just noise — so it's never compared against a good-contact session as if the two were equivalent.

6

Many records become a trend

Plotted session by session over weeks, the same metric can finally show whether recovery looks like it's improving, plateauing, or worth flagging to a clinician — instead of relying on a felt sense of "seems a bit better than last week."

Specs / About

A wearable peripheral nerve recovery tracking glove, built as a hands-on science and engineering project with a real-world medical application in mind.

EMG sensing

Single channel, 3 contacts total — 2 active electrodes plus 1 reference — read through a MyoWare-style EMG sensor.

Flex sensor

Mounted on the index finger, spanning the DIP joint (the last knuckle nearest the fingernail) — the joint most directly affected by nerve signal to that finger.

Form factor

Fingerless compression glove, forearm to knuckles, with a thumb opening. Compression is firm-but-not-tight — just enough to keep electrodes flush against skin without restricting blood flow.

Calibration method

Baseline is captured on-skin, per person, at the best and firmest electrode placement — not one fixed threshold for everyone, since skin conductivity varies a lot from person to person.

Verdict scale

Every contact reading resolves to one of five plain verdicts — None, Poor, Marginal, Good, Excellent — judged against your own calibrated baseline, averaged over repeated samples.

Interface

Connects over Web Serial directly from the browser tab — no app install. Requires Chrome or Edge on desktop (Web Serial doesn't run on Safari or iPhone). Default baud rate 9600, adjustable in the test menu.

Why this project

Musicians like Augustin Hadelich and Hilary Hahn have both dealt with real nerve, tendon, or disc injuries mid-career, and recovery is usually tracked by feel — how a passage feels to play — rather than by any consistent measurement. Existing products, like the MusicGlove hand-therapy device, don't cover this specific gap: an ongoing, personal record of contact-verified signal and reaction data over the course of a recovery. This project is an attempt to close that gap with something wearable and low-cost.

The next real-world step is a conversation with a hand surgeon, physical therapist, or occupational therapist, to sanity-check electrode placement and the reaction-time test design against how nerve recovery is actually assessed clinically.

Nothing typed or measured here leaves the browser tab — there's no account, no server, and no cloud storage. Data shown on the Data page is sample and simulated only, clearly labeled as such, until session logging is actually built.
